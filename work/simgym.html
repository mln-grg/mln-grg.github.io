SimGym — scenario and patient designer

SimGym is a medical training simulator that runs on Meta Quest 2 and 3 and on PC VR. Every new training scenario used to be an engineering task: someone described what they wanted, it went in a backlog, and weeks later they got something close. I built the mode that took us out of that loop, so an instructor sets up the case, picks the patient and dresses the room themselves.

Company
i3 Simulations
Engine
Unreal Engine
Platforms
Meta Quest 2 and 3, PC VR
Users
Instructors, not engineers

The problem

A training simulator lives or dies on the breadth of its content. One scenario is a demo, forty scenarios is a product. But scenario content was authored in engine by programmers, which meant the people who actually understood the training, instructors and subject matter experts, could only describe what they wanted and wait. Every iteration cost a round trip through engineering, and the total content the product could hold was capped by how much programmer time it could buy.

What an instructor actually does

The designer runs as four steps, and none of them require knowing what an actor or a blueprint is.

  • Scenario setting. What kind of case this is, and the data and modifications that go with it.
  • Scenario rules. What counts as correct, and what the simulation should do in response.
  • Environment. The room, and everything in it.
  • Patient. Filter the metahuman library down by the attributes the case needs, then pick one. Training on the same face every time teaches the face.

Dressing the room is the hard part

Placing equipment is where a freehand editor falls apart in the hands of a non engineer. A defibrillator that floats two centimetres above the trolley, a drug that ends up inside a drawer rather than in it, a monitor facing the wall: all of it looks fine in the editor and all of it breaks the moment someone reaches for it in VR.

So placement is constrained rather than free. Surfaces carry their own grids, the floor, the trolley tops, the inside of each drawer, and an object placed on one snaps to that surface in a valid orientation. It turns "arrange this correctly" from a judgement call into a set of legal moves, which is what makes the tool usable by the people who should be using it.

Two headsets and a PC

Everything above ships to Quest 2, Quest 3 and PC VR from one build path. The standalone headsets set the budget: what an instructor is allowed to place, how much of it can be in a room at once, and how much the authoring UI itself is allowed to cost while the scene is loaded behind it.

Why it mattered

  • It changed who could make content. Instructors author their own simulations. Engineering stopped being the bottleneck on the product's most valuable axis.
  • It changed iteration speed. Adjusting a scenario went from a ticket to a session, which means scenarios get refined rather than shipped as first drafts.
  • It is the same instinct as my UI framework work. Given a choice between building a feature and building the thing that lets other people build features, I have repeatedly chosen the second, and on both projects it was the decision that aged best.
