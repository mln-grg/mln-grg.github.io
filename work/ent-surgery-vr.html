ENT Surgery VR — recording & grading

A surgical training simulator is only half a product. The other half is evidence: what the trainee did, whether it was right, and something an instructor can review afterwards. I built that half.

Session recording, in engine

Trainees needed their sessions captured for review. I built the recorder on ffmpeg, driven from the running Unity application, with multi-session management so a machine used all day by successive trainees keeps their recordings separate, identified and retrievable rather than producing one enormous unusable file.

Recording out of a real-time application is a performance problem before it is a video problem. The simulation cannot stutter because encoding is happening — in VR especially, a frame hitch is not a cosmetic issue, it is a comfort and safety one. The work was mostly in keeping capture off the critical path and making the encoder's cost predictable.

Analytics and instructor grading

Recording shows what happened. Grading says whether it was correct. I built the layer that captures what the trainee actually did during a procedure and presents it to an instructor as something reviewable and scoreable — which is what converts a simulator from a practice toy into an assessment instrument, and is usually what a customer is actually buying.

The design question that dominated: what to record. Capture too little and the instructor cannot judge anything; capture everything and you have an unreadable log and a storage bill. The useful unit turned out to be the decision, not the frame.

Load times

Significantly improved, and worth listing because of who felt it. A simulator used in teaching sessions is started and restarted constantly; every second of load is multiplied by every trainee in the room and paid in front of an audience. This is the kind of optimisation that never appears in a feature list and changes how a product feels to use.
