Blacksmith

A browser game in the mould of Flipline's Jacksmith: you are the smith, not the hero. Soldiers queue up outside the forge and tell you what they want. You cast it, beat it into shape, put an edge on it, fit it out, then watch them march off and find out whether your work was good enough.

The loop

Take a job from the campaign map. Read the order — every soldier has a weapon type, a favourite metal, a favourite gem and a favourite shape, and matching them is worth real stats. Then craft, in four stages:

  • Cast. Hold to pour, and move the cursor to control how far the crucible tips. A hard stream folds air into the metal and splashes; a gentle trickle runs clean. The melt is setting the whole time, so dawdling makes the cast porous too. There is a sweet spot in the middle.
  • Forge. Beat the lumps out of the hot blank. Lumps sit on the spine and the edge, so the hammer moves in both axes. Steel cools as you work and cold steel barely moves, so it goes back in the fire — but every reheat costs you.
  • Grind. Optional. Run the edge along the wheel keeping pressure in the green. Too light and it never sharpens; too heavy and you grind the blade away.
  • Fit. Pick a grip, guard, pommel and gem, then place each one. The part hangs from the tongs and swings, so you settle it over the mark and click.

Then the squad auto-fights three waves, you click the ore and coins that drop, and you get graded on who came home alive.

The part worth explaining

A mould is two edge functions over t ∈ [0,1], and everything samples that same silhouette. The pour fills it, the anvil puts lumps in it, the grindstone sharpens its edges, the battlefield swings it. The blade you cast is the blade your soldier carries.

profileAt() is where the craft becomes geometry. It takes the mould's shape, thins it wherever a chamber was under-filled, and swells it wherever un-hammered lumps remain. Mistakes are not a hidden multiplier applied to a stat — they are the outline of the object. Chambers become colour bands, a splashy pour becomes visible pitting, hammering flattens the bulges back out in real time, and grinding writes per-segment sharpness that shows up as edge highlights.

Measured end to end on a three-chamber broadsword, with lumps hammered flat so only the geometry is left. A clean blade is 0.92 half-width at mid-blade:

Pour Cast Lumps at the anvil Mid-blade once flattened
careful 1.00 6 0.908 — full thickness
held too long (1.5×) 0.00 16 (capped) 0.920 — recoverable, but a long shift
stingy (0.68×) 0.63 6 0.733 — permanently 20% thinner

Hot metal is rendered by blending the whole cross-section onto a temperature ramp rather than tinting the alloy colour, with the bevels driving local temperature, so glowing steel still reads as a shaped object instead of an orange blob.

Content and tuning

Eleven ores, each with its own pour viscosity, working heat, hardness and anvil ring. Eighteen moulds across swords, axes, bows and shields. Nineteen fittings and seven gems with elemental effects. Eight soldiers, twelve enemy types, three bosses, ten campaign levels.

Appending ?dev=1 exposes a debug harness: step frames manually, jump to a scene, render a contact sheet of every mould, run a battle headlessly, or simulate the whole campaign at a given craft quality. That last one, dev.sweep, is how the difficulty curve was tuned. Near-flawless crafting clears all ten levels at three stars; decent crafting clears nine and loses the finale; sloppy crafting hits a wall at level four.

No asset files

Every sprite is drawn with 2D path commands and every sound is synthesised with WebAudio at runtime. There is no build step and no framework. It runs off a small Node static server only because the game is ES modules.
