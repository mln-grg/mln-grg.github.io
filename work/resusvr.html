ResusVR — interaction, localisation and analytics

A resuscitation training simulator in Unity. I built the VR interaction systems and a spread of clinical scenarios, then two things that turned it from a demo into a product that could ship anywhere: a localisation system that translates the procedure and not just the words, and an analytics layer that tells you how you actually did.

Interaction first

In a resuscitation sim the interaction layer is the product. If picking up a stethoscope, finding a pulse or reaching for the right vial feels approximate, nothing built on top of it is worth measuring. So the first work was the interaction systems: what can be grabbed, what it does when you have it, what the patient does in response, and how any of that reads through a headset at arm's length.

Localising a procedure, not a string table

The obvious version of localisation is a dictionary: swap the labels, ship the language. That is not enough here, and the reason is on the trolley above. A hospital in one country reaches for a different drug than a hospital in another, and the order of the steps is not always the same either. Translate only the words and you have shipped a simulator that teaches the wrong procedure in a convincing accent.

So the locale drives the scenario, not just its text. A scenario plays out differently, the medications available differ, and it scores differently, because the correct sequence in that locale is a different sequence. The same case is genuinely a different exam depending on where you are sitting.

The pipeline, automated

Localisation was a manual round trip: export, send, receive, re-import, verify, discover the strings that were missed, repeat. It cost real money per release and, worse, cost time on the critical path of every release.

I connected the localisation dictionary directly to Google Sheets. Translators and clinical reviewers work in the sheet, the project pulls from it, and the export and re-import steps stop existing. The measurable result was less turnaround time and less cost on every release afterwards. The structural result was that adding a language stopped being a decision and became a setting.

Analytics, from an event log

A trainee finishing a scenario needs to know more than pass or fail. They need to know which step was late, which one was out of order, and which one never happened.

Everything the trainee does is written as an event into a SQLite store as the session runs. Scoring is then a set of queries over that log rather than a pile of flags maintained by the gameplay code: did this assessment happen, did it happen before that intervention, did it happen inside the window it needed to. That separation is what makes the scoring auditable. When a clinician disagrees with a score you can show them the events and the query, instead of arguing about what the game thought it saw.

Nobody demos a localisation pipeline. It was still the highest leverage thing I could build on that project, because it removed a recurring cost from every future release rather than a one off cost from this one. The analytics work is the same shape: the score is a consequence of the log, so improving the scoring never means touching the gameplay code.
