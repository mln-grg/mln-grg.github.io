Hold of Whiterun

A town of 42 people who each have somewhere to be, an opinion of everyone else, and a memory for slights. A personal project, built to find out how much social texture you get from rules simple enough to hold in your head — and, unlike my day job, one whose source anybody can read.

Type
Personal project
Built with
TypeScript
Population
42 simulated NPCs
Source
On GitHub

The question behind it

Most game towns are set dressing: villagers walk a loop and say one of three lines. The interesting alternative is a town where behaviour is produced rather than authored — where the blacksmith avoids the miller for a reason that happened, in the simulation, on a day you could point to.

I wanted to know how cheap that is. Not "how would a studio build Dwarf Fortress", but: what is the smallest set of rules that makes a town feel inhabited rather than staged?

How it works

Every NPC carries a daily schedule, a set of opinions — one number per other resident — and a list of grudges, which are opinions with a cause attached and a much longer half-life. The simulation advances in ticks. Each tick, an NPC does whatever its schedule says it should be doing, other NPCs present observe it, and observation nudges opinion. Once opinion crosses a threshold, it starts to override the schedule: people go out of their way to be near someone, or out of their way not to be.

What it demonstrates

  • Emergence is a data problem. The behaviour people find interesting comes from what state each agent keeps and who is allowed to observe it — not from the sophistication of any single decision routine.
  • Persistence is what makes it read as narrative. An opinion that resets is noise. A grudge that survives a week of simulated time is a character trait, and it costs one extra field to store.
  • Small, complete, and readable. The whole thing is public. It exists partly because my strongest professional work is under an NDA, and an interviewer who wants to see how I actually write code should have something to open.
